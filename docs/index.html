Overview

A patient-specific antibiogram, at the moment a culture is ordered

Organism identification and susceptibility testing can take several days, so clinicians often choose empiric antibiotics before results are available. During that interval they rely on the hospital antibiogram: the share of recovered isolates that were susceptible to each antibiotic, reported once for all patients.

ARMD-Predict estimates the same quantity for one patient. From information recorded up to the culture order, it estimates the probability that all tested organisms recovered from the culture will be susceptible to each candidate antibiotic. The models were trained on 7.4 million culture–antibiotic results from 415,952 culture orders in 241,227 patients at Stanford Health Care, East Carolina University Health (ECUH), UT Southwestern Medical Center (UTSW) and Mass General Brigham (MGB). Separately trained and evaluated models predict the likely organism and organism-specific resistance phenotypes, including proxies for categories in the 2024 IDSA guidance.

Hospital antibiogram

One number for every patient

For ceftriaxone in inpatient urine cultures, the training-period antibiogram assigned 13.6% resistance to every patient, whatever their history. It cannot separate resistant from susceptible orders.

ARMD-Predict

An estimate for this patient

The estimate draws on the patient's own record: earlier resistant cultures, recent antibiotics, care setting, age band and neighborhood deprivation. Two patients on the same ward can receive different estimates for the same antibiotic.

How it works

Two components built on one set of pre-order features

Both components use information recorded up to the culture order: demographics, care setting, prior resistance and infection, prior antibiotic exposure, prior procedures, comorbidities, laboratory values and the Area Deprivation Index.

COMPONENT 1

Empiric susceptibility

One XGBoost model per antibiotic, specimen and care setting returns the probability that the organisms recovered from the order are susceptible to that antibiotic.

  • 23 pooled four-site models: 7 inpatient urine, 7 outpatient urine, 7 inpatient blood and 2 respiratory.
  • Released models omit site identity. At MGB, the only hospital in this comparison, a site-agnostic pooled model performed nearly identically to a site-aware one.
  • The public web app uses a short form restricted to five feature blocks a clinician can report at ordering: demographics, care setting, prior resistance, prior infection and prior antibiotic exposure.
COMPONENT 2

Organism and resistance phenotype

Three stages address Gram class, organism identity and organism-specific resistance. Each stage was trained and evaluated separately, not as an end-to-end cascade.

  • Gram stain: pooled held-out AUROC 0.72.
  • Organism identification: the 20 most frequent organisms per specimen and setting, plus Other.
  • 30 phenotype models aligned with the 2024 IDSA guidance: proxies for ESBL production and CRE, measured resistance in organisms at moderate risk of AmpC production, and non-susceptibility of P. aeruginosa to individual agents, with MRSA and VRE as Gram-positive complements. AUROC ranged from 0.519 to 0.834, evaluated given the laboratory-reported organism. Phenotype models are not shown in the public web app.

Design choices that keep the estimates sensible

Prior resistance cannot lower predicted resistance

Monotonic constraints apply to prior resistance, prior infection and overall prior antibiotic exposure. A patient with an earlier resistant culture is never scored as less likely to be resistant than an otherwise identical patient without one.

No same-day history

History records dated the same day as the culture were excluded at every site, because whole-day dates cannot show that the event came before the order. Two exceptions: nursing-home contact, whose records mostly carry the culture day, and laboratory summaries, which cover the 14 days up to and including the day of the order.

No patient in two partitions

Each patient's orders fall in exactly one of the training, validation and test partitions (a random patient-grouped 60/20/20 split).

Compared with repeating the patient's last result

A simple alternative to a model is to repeat the patient's most recent earlier result for the same antibiotic, falling back to the antibiogram when there is none. For a median of 36.0% of test orders such a result existed (interquartile range, 28.0–41.4%), so the rule had no earlier result for 64% of orders.

Calibration

Calibration asks whether predicted probabilities agree with how often susceptibility is actually observed. For the ceftriaxone inpatient urine model, raw output was already close to calibrated overall (expected calibration error, 0.004) and at each site (0.004–0.013), and an isotonic transform did not improve it.

Exploratory operating points

Two exploratory thresholds link a probability to a prescribing question. A high threshold identifies susceptible cultures, requiring that at least 95% of the cultures above it are susceptible (positive predictive value of at least 95%); a low threshold flags resistant cultures while flagging no more than 20% of susceptible ones. For ceftriaxone in inpatient urine cultures, 54.9% of susceptible orders were identified at the high threshold and 60.1% of resistant orders were flagged at the low threshold.

Across the four hospitals

Every hospital contributed training data and was evaluated on its own share of the held-out split. For the site-aware development model, median AUROC across 28 inpatient urine antibiotic–site cells was 0.765. For the 23 pooled models in Table 1, across 87 reportable site-specific cells, hospital-specific median AUROC ranged from 0.691 to 0.715.

Subgroup audit

For each model, the audit measured the spread between the highest and lowest AUROC across groups. Restricting to subgroups with at least 1,000 cultures reduced the median age-band range from 0.07 to 0.02, so small groups drove the largest differences.

Use your local antibiogram

Re-anchor to your hospital without retraining

A hospital with more resistance than the training sites would see estimates that are systematically too optimistic, even when the model ranks patients well. A prior-probability correction moves every estimate to the local resistance rate.

  1. Take the local resistance rate for the antibiotic, for example from your hospital's antibiogram.
  2. Each patient's odds of resistance are multiplied by the same factor: the local odds of resistance divided by the training odds. Patients keep their order, so discrimination (AUROC) is unchanged by construction.
  3. The correction assumes that only the overall resistance rate differs between hospitals, not the relationship between a patient's record and their result.
logit(plocal) = logit(pmodel) − logit(πtrain) + logit(πlocal)
Controlled prevalence sweep. At a 40% local resistance rate, median expected calibration error fell from 0.20 to 0.06 after re-anchoring, without changing discrimination.
Leave-one-site-out. Among site–model cells whose prevalence differed from training by at least 3 percentage points, re-anchoring reduced calibration error in 4 of 5 (mean, 0.061 to 0.037).
Caveat. The retrospective analysis used each held-out site's observed prevalence, which is stronger information than a hospital would usually have in advance. The web app's experimental local adjustment is a different, simpler method: it shifts each estimate by the difference between the local and training E. coli resistance rates, weighted by the share of that culture group's organisms that are E. coli. It has not itself been evaluated.
